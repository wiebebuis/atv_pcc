## Introduction
This article is based on a lecture given at the Prince Claus Conservatoire on November 19th 2024. I was invited by Kristina Vårlid, professor of classical guitar. The aim was to share my personal journey with Nocturnal Op. 70 by Benjamin Britten and give my audience the opportunity to look over the shoulder of a music theorist while he/she is working to come to understand ("verstehen") the musical composition at hand. In the course of this article I will take the liberty to share my opinions and make general remarks about the approach I have developed over the years as a music theorist. Not to claim anything, but just to share a possible way to enhance understanding of music in its context. 

Nocturnal Op. 70 is considered to be one of the most important 20th century compositions for guitar solo. It had its premiere by Julian Bream on June 12th 1964 at the Aldeburgh festival, a festival that was initiated and organised yearly by Benjamin Britten and his partner Peter Pears. The subtitle "after John Dowland" refers to the twentieth song from Dowlands "First Book of Songs or Ayres" (1597): "Come, Heavy Sleepe" (CHS), which is the song upon which the composition is based.

## Part 1: General info from the score
A first glance on the score shows us that we are dealing with a composition in eight movements of which the last (Passacaglia) contains the incomplete (instrumental) citation of the song "Come, heavy Sleepe" (CHS) by Dowland in a key that suits the guitar (E major). However, this choice for E major creates an interesting catch as we will see later.

The movements are titled:
1. Musingly (2' 12") (timing of the recording by Julian Bream)
2. Very agitated (0' 57")
3. Restless (2' 00")
4. Uneasy (1' 32")
5. March-like (1' 37")
6. Dreaming (1' 54")
7. Gently rocking (0' 55")
8. Passacaglia - followed by Slow and quiet (Dowland song) (4' 54") + (2' 23")
The duration of movement 8 takes approximately half of the total time of the piece.

https://open.spotify.com/track/0CrcpfltmKpnxjszbPIkB1?si=18ae8458091542ad (This links to Musingly)

Based on this first glance Nocturnal seems to be a collection of variations on a theme in "reverse order": the theme appears at the very end of the composition and creates a sense of "home-coming", coming (literally) to rest, with an open ending: the B-part of the song is not complete and is (thus) not repeated. The composition ends with a "fade out". The titles of the movements are an indication of different characters or moods that can be expected. 

### The title 
**Nocturnal** refers to **night** and relates to the genre **nocturne** in music. It also refers to the Dowland song, of course. In the eighteenth century the Italian term **notturno** was broadly used, and the same goes for the German term **Nachtmusik**. 

Nowadays we relate the label nocturne mostly to a romantic character piece for piano solo as they were composed by John Field and Frédéric Chopin. If we think of "night music" in general, to be performed during the evening or even night, then GROVE learns that a lot of different kinds of night music have been composed by:
Mozart: Eine kleine Nachtmusik and Serenades; Mendelssohn: Nachtstücke; but also Liszt, Bizet, Grieg, Fauré, d'Indy, Glinka, Balakirev, Tchaikovsky, Rimsky-Korsakov and Skryabin. 

And let's not forget the **night music episodes** in the music of Mahler and Bartók; and **Pierrot Lunaire** by Schoenberg. In the era of (later) romanticism also the more mysterious and "creepy" aspects of night and darkness were explored: dreams, visions, hallucinations, nightmares, ghost and witch scenes...
There are even nocturnes for orchestra: by Bizet and Debussy.

### Britten, night music and Dowland
Britten had already composed "nocturnes" before Nocturnal: 
- in Serenade for tenor, horn and strings (Op. 31) mvt. 3, 1943
- Nocturne for 7 instruments and tenor (Op. 60) (1958)
- Night Piece (Notturno) for piano solo (1963)
- Sonatina romantica, mvt. 2, Nocturne (somewhere between 1939 and 1943)

He also composed another instrumental work based on a song by Dowland, "Lachrymae Op. 48(a): Reflections on a song by John Dowland". In this case the song "Flow My Teares" (FMT) was used. There is a similarity between the two: Lachrymae is also a set of variations (although Britten calls them reflections) from which the Dowland song emerges at the end. [1] 

This means we shall have to say something about **intertextuality** too. [2]

## Part 2: General observations on Dowland and Britten in their time

### John Dowland (1563-1626)
Dowland was a famous English lute player and composer, especially of **lute songs**. He spent much of his working life on the continent. He is recognized as the greatest English composer of lute music and lute songs. He published three books of songs, published in 1597, 1600 and 1603. Instead of separate part books he devised a new folio printing format, that could be placed on a table to be read by the performers grouped around it. [image]
#### Dowland's time 
Dowland lived in a musically and culturally turbulent and pivotal time: during the late renaissance and the early baroque era. Around 1600 the opera genre appears on the scene in Italy, and with regard to the used "pitch system" music is in transition from **modality** to **major-minor tonality**. It is also the time of the famous English virginalists. And it is the time of widespread composition genres based on variation and variation techniques (like figuration and diminution): Ricercar, Theme and variations, Fantasia, Prelude, Chaconne and Passacaglia.

The political climate in England was very dynamic; he witnessed the later years of the Tudor-era (Elizabeth I) and the beginning of the era of the House of Stuart (Jacob I who was already king of Scotland as Jacob VI). Also with regard to religion the situation was (very) unstable in the aftermath of the Reformation(s) and the conflict between Henry VIII and the Roman Catholic Church. 
#### Dowland's contemporaries
**Musicians**: William Byrd (1540-1623), Giles Farnaby (1563-1626), Orlando Gibbons (1583-1625), Thomas Weelkes (1576-1623), John Bull (1562-1628). 
**Writers/poets**: Willam Shakespeare (1564-1616), Christopher Marlowe (1564-1593), John Donne (1572-1631), Ben Jonson (1572-1637).
**Scholars**: Galileo Galilei (1564-1642), Tycho Brahe (1546-1601), Johannes Kepler (1571-1630), Robert Fludd (1574-1637).
#### Dowland and melancholy/unhappiness
Quite a few of Dowland's lute songs are rather unhappy and dark in atmosphere. Unhappy love and melancholy made him compose several "weepies":
- Come, heavy Sleepe
- Flow my Teares
- I saw my Lady weepe
- Burst forth, my Teares
- If Floods of Teares
- Sorrow, sorrow
- Weep you no more

In Dowland's time there were several writers who published books on **melancholy** (similar to what we nowadays call depression). The most well known is Richard Burton's The Anatomy of Melancholy (1621). Besides that there are: A Treatise of Melancholy (1586) by Timothy Bright (what's in a name...) and William Perkins' A Whole Treatise of Cases of Conscience (1606). [3]

### Benjamin Britten (1913-1976)
As a child, Britten was immersed in the church music tradition of the Anglican Church.
He was, besides a composer, also a pianist and conductor and witnessed the enormous and unprecedented diversity in musical styles and idioms of the twentieth century. He distanced himself rather resolutely from the continued romanticism of for example Edward Elgar (1857-1934) and Ralph Vaughan Williams (1872-1958). He was heavily influenced by his composition teacher and mentor Frank Bridge (1879-1941). Britten's output as a composer is HUGE. 
Like Stravinsky, Berg, Schoenberg, Messiaen and Bartók he developed an easily recognizable personal style that reflects the diversity of the Western musical landscape of the twentieth century, with musical textures and patterns that enable the listener to orientate comfortably in most of his works. His harmonic language is not straightforward tonal, but tends towards a kind of free extended tonality/free atonality (maybe with the exception of early works and his "Gebrauchmusik" like his famous "Friday-afternoons"). The use of a variety of scale patterns (within one composition) reminds one of Debussy and Messiaen, although the general sound colour of Britten's music distinguishes itself clearly from those composers. 
### The song "Come, heavy sleepe"
#### The poem (text)
The  name of the poet is unknown, we'll call him/her Anonymous.

Come, heavy Sleep, the image of true Death,
And close up these my weary weeping eyes,
Whose spring of tears doth stop my vital breath,
And tears my heart with Sorrow’s sigh-swoll’n cries.
Come and possess my tired thoughtworn soul,
That living dies, till thou on me be stole.

Come, shadow of my end, and shape of rest,
Allied to Death, child to his black-faced Night;
Come thou and charm these rebels in my breast,
Whose waking fancies do my mind affright.
O come, sweet Sleep, come or I die for ever;
Come ere my last sleep comes, or come never.

Both stanzas have the same rhyme scheme: ababcc.

Sleep, Death, Sorrow and Night are written with a capital. As if they are persons, personifications or characters. This is a common practice in the Renaissance and has its roots of course in classical antiquity. **Hypnos** (personification of sleep) is the twin brother of **Thanatos** (personification of death). Their mother is **Nyx** (goddess of the night), their father is **Erebos** (god of darkness). Quite a nice family... [4]

The "weeping eyes" remind us immediately of "Flow, my Teares" from the second book of songs.

#### The song
The melody and lute accompaniment (from the 1597 edition) can be found in Appendix 1 followed by the version from The Elizabethan Songbook.
The songs of the first book are the most simple and straightforward of Dowland's songs (no offense meant, of course). The song consists of an A and B section, the B section is repeated. The overall form schem becomes A-B-B. 

Harmonically there is quite some colour. The tonic of the main key (G major) is in a beautiful way contrasted with an E major and a B major triad. (Our postromantic ear perceives them probably as colourful third-related triads: E and B as mediants of G. Despite the atmosphere of melancholy, sadness (and even controlled despair maybe), the overall key colour is major and not minor. Intriguing...

The lute accompaniment has beautiful and subtle inner voice lines. A nice twist is also realised by what I will call the "false relation notes" c/c# and g/g# (in the original key). Strictly speaking there is no harmonic occurence of false relations. Britten will use them in the transition toward the citation of the Dowland song.

The stepwise descending line in the accompaniment at the beginning, from g' to b (a hexachord), will become the ostinato bass motif in movement 8 of Nocturnal (Passacaglia). 
Appendix 2 presents two charts with the melodic contours to be found in the melody. They can help us to make comparisons/associations  within Nocturnal.
Appendix 3 presents the harmonic outline of CHS.



### Artistic reflections around Nocturnal
It is well known that Britten was not really a fan of music theory and especially music analysis. He shares that opinion with many of his colleague composers. Maybe they like to uphold the (romantic) idea of"divine inspiration", or they are afraid to "kill" the magic or mystery of an art work by analyzing it? I don't know. One nice exception is Olivier Messiaen (1908-1992) who had no secrets and published *Techniques de mon Langage Musical* (1944).

In Britten's own words:

"What is important... is *not* the scientific part, the analysable part of music, but the something that which emerges from it but transcends it, which cannot be analysed because it is not *in* it, but *of* it... it is something to do with personality, with gift, with spirit. I quite simply call it - magic." (Whittall, 1990, p. 10). 

Let the words of Britten speak for themselves. Of course we all have probably experienced magic or even ecstatic moments during concerts. One of mine is the live performance of Mahler's 9th symphony in the Concertgebouw Amsterdam by the Chicago Symphony Orchestra conducted by Solti with the brilliant Adolphe Herseth on first trumpet! (How time flies...).

That being said, it is my (modest) opinion that as a musician and performer we must delve into the way a composer uses his musical material, to discover patterns and principles in order to present a transparent, coherent and expressive performance to the listener. Experimentation (trying alternatives) is (should be...) an important part of working toward a performance. Proportionality is always an important principle. Britten would probably agree, because in his conversations with Julien Bream, he talked regularly of the challenge "how to rethink a musical concept" (YT-video Julien Bream). In the case of Nocturnal it was the musical concept of a theme and variations. So thinking and designing obviously played their role in his process of composing.
#### The journey as a metaphor (process, space & time)
I decided to choose the metaphor of a **journey** for my analysis and lecture about Nocturnal. I made this choice for several reasons:
1. Music is always(?) a process expiring in time, with a beginning, a middle and an end.
2. When listening to music we cannot help to listen for patterns, comparisons, analogies, maybe even narratives, from which some form of understanding can emerge. We are (evolutionally) "wired" in that way. ("Het idee dat de wereld kan worden begrepen aan de hand van patronen en onderliggende principes is een van de belangrijkste inzichten van de mens en misschien wel zijn of haar meest succesvolle overlevingsstrategie." (Rens Bod - *Een wereld vol patronen*))
3. The words we use when talking about music are filled with imagery, visualisations, analogies, attributions and the like. Compare for example the way Charles Rosen writes about music in sonata style as a "tonal drama".
4. My personal experiences while listening to the piece. I share one of them: I (continue to) have a strong sense of "homecoming", harmony, peace , resolution, at the moment we arrive at the Dowland song at the end. (The effect works so strongly because of what precedes the citation, as we will see.)
5. Several times at the end of a movement, there is a kind of announcement or anticipation of the coming movement by using material that will shape the coming variation. So we have a sense of moving from one variation into the next. 
#### Variation and rethinking a (musical) concept
I mentioned already that Nocturnal seems to be a set of variations on a theme in "reverse order".

Variation, as one of the basic form means of music (besides repetition and contrast), is present almost always in music of all cultures and times (probably excluding music that is written to induce a certain (trance-like) state of mind, like "pure" minimal music, meditative music and (sometimes) ambient music). 
Britten was rather fond of variation forms. He applied that concept for example in:
- A Boy was born Op. 3
- Variations on a Theme by Frank Bridge Op. 10
- The Young Persons' Guide to the Orchestra Op. 34
- Lachrymae Op. 48(a)
- Six metamorphoses after Ovid Op. 49 for oboe solo
- Geminini Variations Op. 73
- Suite for Harp Op. 83
- The Cello Suite Nr. 3 Op. 87
- Temporal variations (1936) for Oboe and piano (no Op. number)
#### Associations and analogies ("reflections")
In the case of **Lachrymae** Britten chose the word "reflections" instead of "variations". I think this has to do with the earlier mentioned "rethinking of a concept".  Reflection can mean:
1. thinking about something, and evaluate (for example a reflection paper or report)
2. literally "mirroring", the reflection we see in a mirror for example, but also the way people can mirror behaviour (cf. the working of mirror neurons)
3. the mirror as a metaphor (or even a magic device); not all mirrors give perfect reflections (for example laughing mirrors... ), and sometimes images are blurred, discarded or difficult to perceive  because they come from a distant past (the mirror of Erised and the pensieve in the Harry Potter cycle) or give a hint of future things (the mirror of Galadriel in LotR). We encounter the mirror as a magic device for example in: Little Snow White, Alice in Wonderland, The Beauty and the Beast, Phantastes, The Matrix, and many others.

What can the implications of these meanings be for (composing) music? 

One of my propositions is that Britten is mirroring between his own composition and the song(s) of Dowland. It is "a distant mirror", like the book by Barbara Tuchman with the same title about "the calamitous 14th century". That is why we do recognize melodic contours from CHS in Nocturnal even if they are not aligned to a mostly diatonic major-minor grid. You may compare it to four painted portraits of the same person in different styles: realistic, impressionist, expressionist and cubist: there will be recognition despite differences in technique and appearance of the painting.

When you choose a composer (or one of his/her works) as a starting point or source of inspiration for your own composition, you have to think about the way you can integrate this music from the past into (your) music of the present. In the case of Nocturnal the Dowland material had to be recognisable in one way or another, while at the same time leaving space for a new composition. We wil see that Britten modeled his variations for an important part on **melodic contours** of the Dowland song and that he treats musical parameters other than pitch rather freely, but in a consistent way.

#### Music and art in general
Without becoming to deeply involved in philosopical musings ;-) about why there is art and what the meaning of art is for mankind, I present just my (simple) personal perspective. For me, the arts (and especially music) are a way to enter a kind of "parallel universe" where we can experience beauty and consolation (Cf. Wim Kayzer - On Beauty and Consolation). An alternative universe with its own categories of "time" and "space", freedom and "laws" if you wish. The experiences of beauty and consolation can help us to cope in the "real world".  I know that they help me. Probably I am an incurable romantic. That's ok with me.
#### Music theory and analysis: why and how?
Music theory should serve music and our experiences with music. So it should not prescribe or even dictate how music should be heard or evaluated, but rather approach music with an open detective-like research attitude: What do I hear? What makes the music coherent, or why seems there to be a lack of coherence? What kind of experiences gives the music to me? How does that work? Can I find out why? (Of course (listening) attitude plays a great role. Listening to a Bruckner symphony with the mindset of a jazz musician dealing with a bebop standard (or the other way around...) is probably a guarantee for confusion or disappointment...).
Involving personal experiences with the piece, means that our approach of analysis should have a **phenomenological** component. 
	See also[[Fenomenologie]],[[Embodied cognition]] and [[Embodied Mind]].

How can this be achieved? 

For example, by:
- discovering patterns & principles in the use of the musical material, that can be the foundation of a theory
- using patterns and principles to reduce complexity of the music at hand
- applying (analytical) working models (for example as an analogy of iconology in the visual arts; a kind of "sonography" applying the model of Erwin Panofsky, but there are many more possible
- experimenting: what is the effect of changes in tempo & timing, articulation, dynamics and timbre for the musical expression?
- **NB**: using **personal experiences** and **intuition** (mind, body and soul), which means that the approach should be, where possible, embodied and phenomenological
- creating/constructing understanding ("Verstehen", a hermeneutic term) by weaving a **web of meaning**.
#### Orientation and navigation in music
If we consider music to be a kind of entrypoint of a parallel universe, then the question is how to **orientate** ourselves in that universe, and how to **navigate** in it. That is where **perception** and **analysis** come in. They can help to generate or construct a kind of map (mental map or another form of visualisation). Equally important are the personal **experience(s)** you have while being "in the music". 
## Part three: Analysis of Nocturnal
### 0.  Some introductory remarks
Let me start by offering one bit of advice from an "old white male theorist": Take your time to get to know music, without immediately wanting to get results. Listen to it several times without and with a score. Observe what's happening in the music (**and** what is happening with **you**!).

By doing so we can do general observations while we are familiarising ourselves with the music, and we can (most of the time) already exclude tools or techniques that are not likely to give us results. Let me give one obvious example in the case of Nocturnal: tonality and functional harmony. They seem to be absent, except in the citation of the Dowland song. Nevertheless: the way in which we approach the citation of the song in movement 8 gives us a beautiful soft landing when we arrive at the song. The arrival is not sudden or unexpected. We clearly have a sense of direction. How is that achieved? Interesting...

We will use the barlines in the score to determine measure numbers that wille be placed between parentheses ( ). (NB: Pay attention to the end of staves; if they are open (no barline) then the "measure" continues...). When we discuss **segmentation** in the music, the resulting **segments** will be numbered. We will consider segments as **form** **sections**. If we compare segments or sections between movements the labels will consist of a Roman numeral for the movement and an arabic numeral for the section within the movement (for example III.2).

It is time to dive deeper into the music itself, movement by movement: *Attacca!*

### 1. Musingly

	**Images**: Contour chart (1) and (2), annotated score of movement 1: britten nocturnal deel 1 vergelijking met contouren Dowland.

### First impressions, observations and associations

"**Meditativo**" can be seen as a translation of "musingly". It indicates *thinking*, maybe *meditative exploration* of musical material coming from the Dowland song. It also made me think of "*ricercare le corde*", according to GROVE the root meaning of the **ricercare** genre. This was a current genre in Dowland's time, a genre which departs from a musical idea or "**soggetto**" (subject) and uses variation as a key technique and "varietas" as an aesthetic ideal.

My impression of this movement is one of searching, sometimes hesitant, without a clear focus or direction. The dynamic is overall (very) soft: it varies from ppp to pp. There are two familiar sounding harmonies, but without working tonal contextualisation. The main musical content (texture) is melodic-rhythmic; there are only three chordal moments within segment 6 and segment 9.
#### Musical material
What could be the intention/purpose of this movement? 

I propose the following:
"Let's see what we've got in terms of musical material and what we can do with it". But wait a minute: How do you mean "**see**"? It's **dark**. We have to use our *sense of touch*... we are moving in a careful manner, on tiptoes so to speak.

### Movement 1: Prelude? ... Prelude!
This movement functions as a **prelude** in which the essence of the musical material of the coming movements is presented (for the greater part) melodically; there are two segments (6 and 9) in which harmony plays a role. Although we have barlines, there is no time signature and the rhythm is free and in a way "quasi recitativo".

#### Segmentation
This movement has a strophic-like character; there are in total 9 segments, all ending with a long note, comparable to the line endings in the song. Segment 6 and 9 are the only two where harmony plays a role. We have an Eb9 chord without 7th, the F falls to C. There is no sense of resolution and no suggestion of a key; therefore it's better to consider it as a **polychord**:  Fm over E, where G# = Ab. This "clashing" of triads/keys is a common feature in the music of Britten. The E major triad is followed by an A major triad with an added b6, but these triads can not work functionally because the upper voice is conflicting with them.
#### Melody
In this movement the important/characteristic contours from the Dowland song can be easily recognised, but they are distorted. There is no sense of a key. There is a lack of focus and direction in this movement. I think this is a deliberate choice by Britten. Maybe he is paraphrasing a song of Dowland: "In darkness I make you dwell"?

With regard to **pitch**: all 12 pitch-classes are used, selections from these 12 result in recognisable patterns. Those can be general textural patterns like a one-note pattern (tone repetition), a chord pattern (triad, polychord, etc.) a scale pattern, a melodic motif or contour, or a technique like sequencing, a contrapuntal technique like imitation . All are possible ways to present a basic set of patterns with a great variety. 

In contour chart (1) I have tried to point out relations between movement 1 (the "prelude") and Dowlands melodic contours. Segment 1 through 6 can be directly and easily linked to contour 1 through 6.

In contour chart (2) we find general patterns not directly related to the melody: the descending phrygian hexachord from the beginning accompaniment, a subset of that in the form of a descending tetrachord (the FMT-tetrachord), the ascending lydian pentachord, and the descending tetrachord accompaniment motif. All are, in a way, "scale patterns". In a lot of music scales and scale patterns are as common as the air we breathe (or for a fish, the water it swims in). 

The overall form is (in terms of Hermann Erpf) a "Reihenform" or sequencing form: a chain of 9 clearly separated and associated segments. Maybe we could even propose that movement 1 is a prelude in the form of a 20th century variation on CHS material.

### Overview

Commentary to go with annotated page of movement 1:
- Segment 1 =~ contour 1: range is a diminished fifth (or tritone), and not a perfect one, so tonally ambiguous. 
- Segment 2 =~ contour 2: C# can be heard as a temporary "finalis". 
- Segment 3 =~ contour 3, "snake" 1: the stepwise ascending movement resembles the start of contour 3 (again a tritone); there is a sequence which appeared in the Dowland song in contour 7. The ^ signs mark the frame of a whole tone scale, also the tone repetition is present; a bit extended and it marks the tritone b-f.
- Segment 4 =~ contour 4, "snake" 2: the third is missing (filled), the fifth c-g is filled chromatically.
- Segment 5 =~ start contour 1: the (general) interval pattern <3 2 2> is repeated, last note = first note of the next. So a <3 2 2> pattern starting on e, b-flat, and f.
- Segment 6 =~ contour 6 with major triad added (E major will become the arrival tonic in movement 8 of the Dowland song). The rhythmic pattern "come and posess my tired..." is directly quoted.
- Segment 7 =~ contour 5 + sequencing of the start of contour 7, the frame of several diminished triads makes it tonally unstable.
- Segment 8 = somewhat an "odd duck": possibly related to contour 6 (tone repetition) combined with a (general scale pattern) that creates the suggestion of two voices. 
- Segment 9 =~ contour 6: the start of it. The repeated fourth f-c becomes the stepping stone towards movement 2; it is presented four times at the beginning.

### 2. Very agitated
### First impressions and observations
The second movement contrasts strongly with the first:
- in character: very agitated instead of musingly/meditative
- in tempo: fast instead of slow (the impression of speed is amplified by the use of triplets)
- it's a virtuoso movement, with a kind of toccata-character
- the dynamics are mostly loud instead of soft
- there is a sense of urgency/direction instead of (prudent) searching
- the movement is metric and pulse-based instead of rhythmically free
- no rests (except for one negligible eight-note rest in measure 17).
### Segmentation and a composer's"present"
Just as in movement 1 there  is a clear segmentation in (again) 9 segments and a strophic approach (long note at the end of a segment). Each segment ends with a (relatively long) sforzato chord. The chord consists of notes of the open strings. By the way: All strings together form a kind of polychord: the (minor) triad E-g-b-e', the stacked fourths E-A-d-g, and the (major) triad in six-four position d-g-b. This was a kind of present for Britten; the instrument gives him a major and a minor triad and a typical post tonal chord consisting of stacked fourths. So harmonic patterns of past and present are available.Very convenient :-).

	This present is comparable to that of Berg in his violin concerto, a concerto that Britten was very fond of. He attended at least three live performances in 1936, and purchased a full score of it. The tuning of the instrument gives three stacked pure fifths, that can be made into major and minor triads (Gm, D, Am, E) with the fifth of E (B) as the starting point of the remaining three pitch-classses to complete his twelve tone row and becoming the literal bridge to the citation of the Bach chorale (Es ist genug!!) in the form of the tritone motif B-C#-D#-E#/F. 
	It is the only Bach chorale that begins with a stepwise tritone motif (for retorical reasons, because it's about Death). Also here typical tonal patterns from the past (triads) and a key interval of non tonal music of the (then) present: the tritone; the four tones together form the greater part of a whole tone scale.
	<12-tone row Berg violin concerto>
#### Tension
There is also a sense of development and building up of tension; the sforzato chord doesn't feel like a resting point, but rather as the the convincing and resolutely reaching of a goal that is at the same the springboard for the next segment with again the energetic triplets. The overall arc of tension is up long towards the b'' in segment 8, down short.

In the melodic movement we can easily recognize "snake" like contours.

The polychord is also present, but in the form of an arpeggio (segment 6). The rhythmically written out ritardando and the tone repetition function as anticipation/preparation of movement three, where tone repetition becomes interval repetition as a background in the middle register.

There is (again) no sense of tonality, despite the use of some triads in the polychord arpeggio. The pitch collection contains all 12 pitch-classes.

### Overview
- segment 1: the CHS-motif (3 up, 4 down, 2 down)
- segment 2: comparable to contour 2 and 3; steps down, steps up 
- segment 3: comparable to "snake" 1 (contour 3), almost totally chromatic
- segment 4: filled diatonic fifth, comparable to contour 5, feels as a scale based pattern, not really "snake"-like; repetition of notes C and Ab can be linked to contour 6 (tone repetition) in contour 
- segment 5: beginning is comparable to contour 5; 3 up, 2 up, 2up; the stationary note f# combined with a moving "upper voice" reminds of the "odd duck" contour 8
- segment 6: can be linked to contour 6 (polychord)
- segment 7: interval structure reminds of contour 5, also the principle of sequencing (contour 7) is present
- segment 8: tone and interval repetition and the last group of contour 7, which is also related to contour 1
- segment 9: polychord again (segment I.6 and I.9 )

The overall form is again (in terms of Hermann Erpf) a "Reihenform" or sequencing form: a chain of 9  associated segments.  The tone repetition prepares an important charasteric of the accompaniment layer of movement 3. Not tone repetition but interval repetition.
### 3.  Restless
### First impressions and observations
There is a strong contrast with movement 2. 
The dynamics are overall soft (only one time cresc. from f).
For the first time, the overall texture is harmonic-contrapuntal instead of primarily melodic-rhythmic. This movement is, like movement 2, metric and puls-based with a ternary division. We have a 3/4 "feel" although in the score the notated rubato value is a dotted quarter note... (why?). 
Again no rests (meaning no simultaneous rests that involve all three layers).
##### Texture
There is in the middle register a rhythmic-harmonic layer (one is tempted to call it a "tenor"), consisting of repeating harmonic intervals (and sometimes trichords), above and below which two voices move in a more or less contrapuntal fashion with short motives that evolve into longer melodic lines. There is the use of imitation, inversion and sequencing. There is also some kind of dialogue/conversation between the upper and lower voice.

##### Harmony and pitch collection
All twelve pitch-classes are used.

The intervals/trichords of the harmonic rhythmic layer are dissonant or consonant and unstable (use of the perfect fourth).
##### "Rhythmical dissonance"
Although the pulse in the central layer is constant and stable, the composer requires rubato and combines this with antimetrical patterns: 2 against 3, and (more awkward) 3 against 4, which creates what I would like to call "rhythmic dissonance". These are in my opinion the main tools to create a sense of restlessness in this movement. 

	The use of rubato requires some reflection of the performer; the amount of rubato should allow the listener to keep track of the note value that represents the pulse, even if it is played with rubato. The use of rubato should be proportional. Not too mechanic, nor to free. This is also important for the "feel" of the antimetric patterns. Their effect depends on there being some kind of yardstick of time (pulse) in relation to which the antimetric patterns are meant to cause a pleasant disturbance :-) ...
##### Segmentation
As in movement 1 and 2, there is segmentation, but this time it is a less articulated, soft segmentation. Segmentation is realised primarily by change of sonorities in the central layer. Because of the overlap (new sonority under the last note of a melodic segment) and no rests or commas, the overall impression is more fluent than in previous movements. 

The used means of segmentation result in (again) 9 segments.
(See also the texture chart of movement 3).
Segments 7 and 8 are special: in segment 7 the two melodic voices come together (above the rhythmic layer) and in segment 8 the central layer disappears and the two melodic voices realise a note against note counterpoint.
##### Tension
The overall arc of tension is building up until measure 26, Eb5, quickly winding down to pp in measure 30. After that the dynamics remain soft.
### Overview
- Segment 1: a three-note motif in the lower voice (step up, third down) is followed by its inversion in the upper voice
- Segment 2: is comparable to contour 2; "turn" motif, upper voice develops the lower voice and introduces upward sequencing of the descending stepwise third
- Segment 3: comparable to contour 3 ("snake" 1) but only in lower voice
- Segment 4: brings in the upper voice an elaboration of segment 3; comparable to contour  "snake 2", then upward sequencing until the top note of the movement (Eb6, then downward sequencing thirds while realising a "Sekundgang" in a whole-tone scale pattern (Eb-Db-B-A-G), and after that ending with the last three notes of the CHS-motif.
- Segment 5: returns to the texture of segment 1. The lower voice is  followed by an inversion in the upper voice, then the same voice combination follows, but horizontally compressed (last note lower voice coincides with first note upper voice).  The upper voice continues with a descending scale pattern of a fifth.
- Segment 6: also comparable to the texture of segment 1 and 5. Also a horizontal compression (last note lower voice coincides with first note upper voice)
- Segment 7: the two melodic voice move together in contrary motion **above** the "rhythmic harmonic layer"
- Segment 8: the central layer is absent, the two melodic voice realise a note to note counterpoint
- Segment 9: reminds of segment 5 with the use of a fourth and its inversion, in dialogue between lower and upper voice.
### 4.  Uneasy
### First impressions and observations
The general first impression is: fragmentation and sound effects. The movement is (as before, and this is also a pattern) built up of clearly separated segments: five in this movement. No real melodic lines, the most melodic part is the virtuoso scale pattern somewhere in the middle of the movement. Some of the sound effects reminded me of old fashioned radio communication and R2D2 :-). We will encounter them again in movement 8.
https://www.soundsnap.com/tags/r2_d2

We hear short and (very) fast motives, separated by rests. A new effect is introduced: a sfz trichord arpeggio, of which the top note is used for a tremolo, changing into a tone repetition with increasing note values combined with a diminuendo. 

There is one accelerating "virtuoso" passage built from scale patterns. This seems to be the central part of this movement.

After this central part we encounter an approach similar to the beginning: a "new" motif is introduced followed by a new element:  a (left hand) "tremolo third". Also the sfz chords are present. The movement ends with sfz arpeggio pentachords followed by tone repetition, that rhythmically anticipate an important accompaniment motif of the next movement "March-like".
### Overview
If we take a closer look at the different segments the following relations to previous movements can be made:
- Segment 1: the fast motive is comparable to the "CHS-motif (contour 1). It occurs three times,  the third one is an inversion. The tone repetition is a characteristic from contour 6.
- Segment 2: the motif resembles the "turn" motif (contour 2). It occurs two times "straight", two times inverted. The  tone repetition can (again) be traced to contour 6.
- Segment 3: it is not a straightforward ascending and descending scale, but a "snake" 1 variant (only steps). This segment ends with a tetrachord followed by the familiar tone repetition.
- Segment 4: introduces a "new" motif (with the range of a fifth) consisting of steps and a third; it can be related to the beginning of contour 4. Then again the "turn" motif (inverted), followed by the (now ascending) fifth-motive (consisting of steps and one third) which could be related to the beginning of contour 5. The third-tremolo is presented in a sequence, the last tremolo becomes a fourth-tremolo. 
- Segment 5:  can be considered as a "codetta"; two times a sfz pentachord followed by tone repetition

### 5. March-like
### Some associations and expectations
After a restless (3) and uneasy movement (4), it seemed to be time for a more recognisable musical character and texture. The title "March-like" allows us to (maybe) expect some familiar elements from marching music: Tonal harmony, pulse based (binary) rhythm, homophonic texture, chordal accompaniment with a clear bass line and rhythmical articulated chords ('oom-pah" "oom-pah") Sometimes there is one section in which the bass has the "melody" and almost always a more lyric section ("trio") often with a (tenor) melody in inner voices. 
<Example: Spotify-link>

Britten living in Britain :-D was without doubt familiar with brass bands and wind bands, which have marches in their standard repertoire. The use of the important accompaniment motif < Note example > is for me proof enough of that. In the context of this composition it will probably be a "distorted" version of march music; hence the label march-**like**. Which indicates that it probably won't be the easy listening straightforward traditional thing...
It turns out to be a parody on a march.
#### First impressions and observations
We hear a melodic line in octaves, above and below a (central) rhythmic chord layer in irregular time signatures. The association with the texture of movement 3 is obvious. The difference is the absence of contrapuntal elements. The used chord material is again derived from the open strings of the instrument.

As a consequence of the melody in octaves between "bass" and "soprano", there is no separate bass line with a harmonic function (bass note of the harmonies) and metric function (on strong and relatively strong beats. In simple words: there is no "oohm-pa, oohm-pa" pattern.

It's obvious that the composer doesn't want us to get in a groove... :-).

This parody-like approach of the march-concept could be compared with music of Stravinsky for example from the first movement of L'Histoire du Soldat. The glissando is a nice tongue-in-cheek suggestion of a trombone.

### 6. Dreaming
### First impressions and observations
In mood this movement comes the closest to the first movement. Special is the abundant use of harmonics (the only movement by the way). The overall dynamics are soft (p-pp-ppp). The rythm is free, although the score gives an indication of a slow quarter note for the tempo. Like  the first movement, there is no real sense of direction (as a result of many fermatas). This seems to be understandable; a dream is received, it comes to you (or it doesn't) and cannot be forced to happen. So when it comes to you, you move along with the flow of the dream. You are not in the driver's seat...  It's also obvious in my opinion that it is clearly not a nightmare...
### Structure and texture
The movement has 8 clearly separated segments with a repeating colour structure:
ordinary sound-harmonics, ordinary sound-harmonics, etc.
The colour change and textural change are the cues for segmentation.
A higher level order is also possible, if we group the two in the manner of an antecedent-consequent phrase:
I (1+2) - II (3+4) - III (5+6) - IV (7+8). 
Segment 1, 3, 5 and 7 contain hints of the speech rhythm of lines from the poem:
- come, heavy sleep
- and close up these
- come and posess
- till thou on me be stole
Segment 1, 3, 5 and 7 are written in a four-part non-tonal harmony, you could see them as lines from a chorale (or a song...) with a moving inner voice. In the moving inner voice a descending stepwise pattern regularly occurs. So with regard to voiceleading it is totally smooth and plausible. 

< Endnote: Schoenberg stated that a chord can be followed by any chord of choice as long as the voiceleading is smooth (stepwise). From jazz harmony we know that parrallels that are considered problematic in triad based harmony, are less offensive when the grade of dissonance increases, as is the case with seventh chord based harmony. >

A stepwise pattern that we know from the hexachord, the "snake" 2 motive and the FMT tetrachord.
The open-string chord A-d-g-b-(e) is used often: it occurs at the start and end of segment 1, 3 and 7, and once in segment 8.

### Pitch and harmony
The familiarity of the "open-strings chord" of the guitar creates a sense of "home" on the instrument. But it is another home feeling than that of a straightforward tonic triad.
The colour of harmony is mainly determined bij stacked fourths and stacked fifths combined with a third. The A is repeated as lowest note, but does not create the sense of a pedal, firstly because it diminishes every time and secondly because of the continuous dissonance of the overall harmony.
< Endnote: the traditional effect of the pedal point is the bass being alternately consonant and dissonant with the harmonies above it. >

### 7. Gently rocking
### First impressions and observations
"Gently rocking" triggers the association of a lullaby and a rocking cradle. The to and fro rocking movement is represented by the accompaniment open string notes.
The music is, as could be expected from a lullaby, non intrusive, with soft dynamics (pp-ppp).
Typical is the crossing of upper and lower part during this movement.
In the score there is the suggestion of **bitonality** with the double key signature Db major/B-flat minorvs. C major/a minor. 
The same kind of "clashing" we encountered with the polychords in the first movement.

We could call this movement the tremolo-variation, the tremolo can be seen as just another form of tone repetition. With regard to playing technique and texture, the association with **Barrios** (Una limosna el amor de dios) and **Tarrega** (Recuerdos de la Alhambra) is obvious. 

It is a play between two parts/voices/lines. Unpretentious; there is not a lot happening which is logical, that would keep us awake... ;-).

Phrasing: with regard to form and structure there are again clear segments: we have three phrases that end on a relatively long note (measure 8, 14 and 23).
In the melodic line there is an ongoing eight-note movement that is abandoned toward the end.

Contours: In the upper voice the CHS-motif is embedded in the ongoing eight-note movement.
### 8. Passacaglia?
The "passacaglia" that leads to the Dowland song, is the longest and most complex of the movements. Because the passacaglia is in itself a variation-form or -procedure, we have a variation form within a variation form.

When I first heard movement 8 of Nocturnal I did not have a passacaglia-like experience that connected to other passacaglias I'm familiar with. Not at all. Britten's smokescreen was lifted gradually when I became more familiar with the piece.

### Passacaglia contextualised
As a composition genre, the passacaglia is a somewhat problematic concept. In musical practice passacaglia is considered a synonym of chaconne/ciacona. The chaconne has a long history going back to the late 16th century in the South-American colonies of Spain. The history of it continues into the 20th century in music of Ravel, Schoenberg, Bartók, Shostakovich and many others.

In the beginning the chaconne was described as a dance-song of slaves, servants and native people. In the 16th century it became the most popular Spanish dance besides the zarabanda.
The first notated versions come from Italy, but the concept spread throughout Europe. The framework consists of a simple chord sequence. The time signature is almost always ternary.
The descending bass line (8)-(7)-(6)-(5) is used very often and is the same as what later in history becomes used as the Prinner partimento pattern. If this bass line is used in a chromatic version, then it becomes the well known Lamento-pattern. < Endnote: The Prinner can be used with 8-7-6-5 in the bass line but also with 4-3-2-1. If you start with 4-3-2-1, and continue with 8-7-6-5, you can easily modulate to the dominant key. Cf. Job IJzerman's HACOPA). >

As mentioned earlier, the chaconne is used as a synonym of passacaglia. This can give cause to some confusion. The most obvious correspondence between the two is, that they consist of grouped measures (2, 4, 8 or even 16), with a cadence at the end as a (temporary) conclusion. In that way it is a typical "Reihenform" or sequencing form.

### Improvisation
In earlier times improvisation was an important characteristic in the practice of chaconne/passacaglia. The repeating chord sequence was an invitation to do that. The most simple bass line is descending tetrachord in the bass lines: (8)-(7)-(6)-(5). Increasing diminution, ornamentation/figuration, and using (fast) scale patterns was a possibility to demonstrate virtuosity on the instrument. 

The famous Bach passacaglia in C minor is for many people a kind of prototype of the passacaglia concept, but this work stands relatively on its own.

Nocturnal is obviously composed and not improvised; but the unfamiliarity with an approach as Britten used may well give the listener an general impression of improvisation, because there is no expectation-realisation  process, comparable to what we often have in traditional variation forms such as ricercars, fugues, sonatas, concertos, or other musical forms with a generally shared and accepted "working concept".
< Endnote: working concept as in a fairy tale: The first sentence "Once upon a time in a country far, far away...". >

### Passacaglia in Nocturnal
This movement does not remind one immediately of a "traditional" passacaglia. There is no bassline that is immediately repeated, that confirms a key, no clear cadential formulas and a ternary time signature. What we do have is a repeated ostinato bass-motive, exactly the hexachord-motive we discussed in movement 1. Later on this bass motive gets modified ("distorted"). Typically the bass motive is not used as the bass line of upper harmonies, but often placed beneath the longer ending notes of the upper voices. What is similar to the "traditional approach" of the passacgali concept is the way of building up tension by, for example:
- increasing the amount of voices/parts
- increasing the rhythmical density
- increasing dynamics
- increasing pitch range
- increasing harmonic tension and dissonance.
< End note: A famous and exciting passacglia is to be found in Symphony nr. 15 of Shostakovich >

In this composition the passacaglia functions as a preparation of and a bridge toward the Dowland song.
### Segmentation
We have 5 segments or episodes, each with subsegments.
1. measure 1 through 15: 1 upper voice, 2 upper voices (5), 3 upper voices (9), in measure 11 parallel majord thirds, in measure 11 parallel seventh chords which contributes to the building up of tension.
2. measure 16 through 25: dynamics back to p, 1 upper voice and texture change (arpeggio technique and suggestion of contrapuntal lines) sudden ending with ff dynamics.
3. measure 26 through 28: , back to pp and change of "idiom": quasi electronic music approach with three times a twelve tone row (sort of).
4. measure 29 through 34: dotted chord-rhythms in upper voices, intensifying from three-note to six-note upper structures, bass-motive appears in octaves and after that in double octaves.
5. measure 35 through 41: bass-line motif seems to disappear, this is a virtuoso episode with repeated introduction of the E major chord in arpeggio (5 times), the bass-motif (hexachord) is used to construct a chain of hexachordal (19) virtuoso scale patterns, the rhythm slows down and with the slowing down the hexachord becomes gradually more recognisable: in the last two times several "false relation" effects are added: g#/g, and also the c and f work beautifully as dissonants against the b and e of the E major triad. 

Segments 1, 2 and 4 have an upbuilding arc of tension, segment 4 is even and segment 5 winds down or phases out to the pp beginning of the Dowland song. 
Of course different musical parameters can have their own contribution to the building up of tension.
The are the dynamic gradations:
1. ppp-ff
2. p-ff
3. pp
4. pp-p-mp-mf-f-ff
5. ff-mf-mp-p-(pp).

### The "soft landing" on E major
Earlier I wrote something about the soft landing on the start of the Dowland song. From the beginning of Nocturnal Britten avoided tonal patterns or obscured them. The longer this is continued, the stronger becomes the resolution or home-coming effect at the start of the Dowland song. In Nocturnal the home coming is prepared:
- we have a non tonal idiom that moves back from atonal to chromatic to diatonic pitch collections
- we have the repeated introduction of the E major triad
- we have a diatonic pitch collection being moulded into the "phrygian hexachord"
that becomes the accompaniment motif in the first bar of the Dowland song. 

So: **context** is a key factor for the working of an effect. We see that in painting with the effect of colour combinations: the same yellow looks more bright on a dark blue background than on a light grey background. Another analogy: being on a ship in the pacific for six months and then arriving at a beautiful tropical island is more exciting and intense than take the ferry from Harlingen to Vlieland...
Or: crossing a desert for days or week, being short of water,  and then arrive at an oasis and offered delicious cool water... is more intense than drinking your second cold beer of the evening in a pub in Groningen in the winter...

### The Dowland song
The Dowland song is in the key of E major, fitting to the instrument.
The citation is incomplete; the B part is not complete. 
There is an open ending with the falling fourth g# - d# and fade out.
The overall atmosphere is one of rest, peace, tranquility, acceptance (despite the "topic" of the poem...). 

#### The "catch"
Here I want to draw the attention to the catch I mentioned in the beginning, connected to the choice for E major as key of the Dowland song. While listening to Bream the G# triad really annoyed me. This "problematic" major triad on G# sounds not relaxed, approaching a pythagorean third, which is a direct consequence of the key and tuning choices, I suppose.  Is there a way out of this dilemma? Is it possible to "temper" the fourth between the d and g-string, to get a slightly flatter g# on that string? But that has consequences if you use that g# as the root of g# major. The third becomes too wide... A compromise seems unavoidable (is it?). (This is a nice topic for discussion).

#### Closing a circle
The complete composition starts emerging from silence and in the end dissolves back into silence. And at a certain moment we return to "reality", as in a play. We wake up, so to speak. ## Appendix 5: Imagination and visualisation
##### Imagination and visualisation in movement I
You are playing blind man's buff at a birthday party. There is a trail on which you encounter 9 objects, which you have recognise by describing them while using all relevant senses. The trail between the 9 objects is challenging and requires balancing. There are: a guide strip for the blind, a balance beam, a stair, a slide, a ladder, and so on. If you fall or stumble, the consequences can be (very) unpleasant, so you have to be careful...
##### Imagination and visualisation in movement II
Imagine playing a super high speed racing game in which you have to: 
- remain on race-track (the pitch collection of the movement)
- making all the required turns of the track and avoid dangerous obstacles by steering left and right (snake movement)
- make risky jumps (long note at the end of a segment is the jump) with a safe landing back on the track
- at the end you run out of fuel, you slow down and park in the pit.
##### Imagination and visualisation in movement III
Imagine an animation film/artwork in the form of a slowly evolving abstract painting with a background of changing colours, against which two melodic lines move in a kind of dancing pattern: sometimes the lines are interacting, sometimes they dance solo, sometimes they dance together as in a ballroom-dance.
